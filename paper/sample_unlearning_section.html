Section 4.8

Cross-Architecture Replication of the Unlearning Battery: ConvNeXt vs. ViT

A corrected ConvNeXt-Base baseline and a full ten-arm unlearning replication supersede Section 4.7's architectural-dissociation claim with a measured one: ConvNeXt encodes writer identity as strongly as ViT does, but resists having it removed.

4.8.1Experimental Setup

Section 4.7 reported that ConvNeXt-Base's writer-identification accuracy remained near chance under the joint-training recipe used for the ViT-Base (TrOCR) encoder, and read this as evidence of genuine architectural dissociation: that convolutional inductive biases fail to encode writer-specific stylistic features the way self-attention over patch tokens does. A follow-up diagnostic falsifies that interpretation. A fresh linear probe, trained directly on ConvNeXt's frozen, mean-pooled patch embeddings rather than on its jointly-trained writer head, recovers 77–87% writer accuracy across the forget, retain-control, and test-reference splits — far above the chance floor and close to ViT's own probe accuracy. The near-chance result in Section 4.7 traces to an implementation detail, not a representational one: the writer head read out a learned CLS-token parameter that was never wired into the convolutional feature path, so it saw no writer-discriminative signal regardless of how strongly the underlying representation carried one.

We repaired the architecture — replacing the inert learned CLS token with a mean-pooled summary of the patch grid, computed fresh on every forward pass, so the writer head reads the representation the encoder actually produces — and retrained the joint recognition/writer-identification baseline from scratch under an otherwise unchanged recipe (writer-loss weight λw = 2.0, single AdamW parameter group, matching schedule). The corrected checkpoint reaches 98.07% / 99.00% forget/retain writer accuracy and 1.00% / 0.82% forget/retain CER: writer-identification performance now comparable to ViT's, with recognition quality intact.

With a functioning baseline in hand, we replicated the full ten-arm sweep of Table 1 — Stage-I pruning followed by WIC, Random Labeling, Fisher Forgetting, Amnesiac Unlearning, and DELETE (Kang et al., 2026), then ARF and MGDA-weighted WIC/Random Labeling (Wang et al., 2026) — against the corrected ConvNeXt checkpoint, using the identical forget/retain/test writer split (20 / 74 / 30 writers), the identical evaluation protocol (greedy decoding, num_beams=1, throughout), and the identical audit instruments. The only architecture-specific change was Stage-I's pruning-target selection: ConvNeXt-Base has no uniform transformer stack to split evenly into shallow/middle/deep thirds, so its four native stages (block depths 3/3/27/3) were grouped as shallow (stages 1–2), middle (stage 3), and deep (stage 4) — an uneven split matching ConvNeXt's own uneven depth distribution rather than forcing ViT's even one.

4.8.2Results

Table 2 reports both encoders side by side under identical procedure. Two results hold across both architectures. First, every method's qualitative behavior replicates: Fisher Forgetting destroys the retain set on both encoders (0.8% ViT / 0.0% ConvNeXt retain accuracy, CER diverging on both) rather than selectively forgetting, reproducing the failure mode reported by the method's introducing work; MGDA-weighted objectives fail to outperform their fixed-weight counterparts on either encoder, and do markedly worse on ConvNeXt; and ARF's representation-level attack does not clearly beat output-level WIC on either backbone.

Second — and invisible to a single-architecture table — ConvNeXt is measurably harder to forget from. Every one of the nine non-baseline arms leaves more residual forget-writer signal on ConvNeXt than the identical procedure leaves on ViT, by 11.0 percentage points on average (minimum +1.1, Random Labeling; maximum +24.5, DELETE), despite near-identical baseline writer accuracy on both encoders (100% ViT vs. 98.07% ConvNeXt). The gap is not explained by ConvNeXt starting from a weaker signal: it starts from essentially the same place and simply gives up less of it.

We read this as the corrected, positive counterpart to Section 4.7's retracted claim. ConvNeXt-Base does not fail to encode writer identity; it encodes it about as strongly as ViT-Base does, and is consistently more resistant to having that identity removed by every unlearning procedure tested here. This is a genuine architectural effect — but one about the difficulty of forgetting, not about representational capacity, and it strengthens rather than undermines this paper's central audit finding: output-level unlearning leaves recoverable writer identity behind, and that conclusion now holds across two structurally unrelated encoders.
