Reading the Result

Both encoders agree on which methods work. Fisher Forgetting collapses on both (0.8% retain on ViT, 0.00% on ConvNeXt, CER blowing up on both) — the failure mode is a property of the method, not the encoder. MGDA weighting fails to beat its fixed-weight counterpart on both, too.
But ConvNeXt is measurably harder to forget from — every single time. All nine non-baseline arms show ConvNeXt retaining more forget-writer signal than ViT after the same procedure, by +11.0 points on average. The gap is largest for shock-based methods (DELETE: +24.5 pts, WIC+MGDA: +21.0 pts) and smallest for simple relabeling (Random Labeling: +1.1 pts).
The gap isn't explained by a weaker baseline. ConvNeXt's baseline writer-ID (98.07%/99.00%) is close to ViT's (100%/100%) — both near ceiling. Something about ConvNeXt's representation resists these specific unlearning procedures more than ViT's does, rather than simply having less to forget in the first place.
This replaces the manuscript's dissociation claim with a sharper one. Section 4.7's "genuine architectural dissociation" (ConvNeXt doesn't encode writer identity) is false — it was a pooling bug. The real, now-measured finding: ConvNeXt encodes writer identity just as strongly as ViT, but is more resistant to having it removed by every unlearning method tested.
